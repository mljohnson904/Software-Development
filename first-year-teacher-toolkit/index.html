First-Year Teacher Toolkit

Practical checklists, templates, and scripts for your first year. Your progress saves in this browser only.

Welcome. You do not have to know everything yet.

Nobody walks into year one fully prepared. Experienced teachers had the same first-year panic. What matters most is:

  1. Build predictable routines so students know what to expect.
  2. Build relationships so students want to work with you.
  3. Plan one day at a time, then one week at a time.
  4. Ask for help early, before a problem grows.
Give yourself permission: Your lessons will be rougher than you want. Your room will look unfinished. You will forget something. Those are normal and fixable.

Your first three priorities

1. Know the basics

  • Bell schedule, lunch, and recess times
  • Where the copier, supplies, and fire exits are
  • Your login info for grades, email, and attendance
  • Who your mentor or go-to colleague is

2. Know your students

  • Check the class roster for students with IEPs or 504 plans
  • Learn names and how to pronounce them
  • Look for allergies or medical notes in the office system
  • Find out who to contact about each student's needs

3. Make the first week boring on purpose

Routines are the most valuable thing you can teach in the first weeks. Teach how to enter, sharpen pencils, turn in work, ask for help, and leave. Practice until it is automatic.

Quick tips that save you real time

  • Do not grade everything. Pick a few key assignments. Use quick checks (exit tickets, thumbs up, whiteboards) for daily feedback.
  • Set a work cutoff time. Teaching expands to fill all available hours. Decide when your workday ends and protect it.
  • Save everything you make. Create a shared folder by unit. Next year's you will be grateful.
  • Document interactions. Write a short note whenever you contact a parent or have a serious behavior conversation. Include date, what happened, and what you said.
  • Find a friendly, veteran teacher and ask them, "What do you wish someone had told you in year one?"

The first-week plan

      
Spend most of the first few days teaching procedures, not content.

      

        

          
Day 1: Arrive and settle

          
    
            
  • Stand at the door and greet every student
    • 
            
  • Show a bell-ringer or entry task on the board
    • 
            
  • Introduce yourself briefly and state two or three expectations
    • 
            
  • Practice entering and leaving until it is smooth
    • 
          

        

        

          
Days 2 to 5: Teach the systems

          
    
            
  • Bathroom and water procedures
    • 
            
  • How to get help when you are stuck
    • 
            
  • Turning in and collecting work
    • 
            
  • Transitions, lining up, and clean-up
    • 
            
  • Signal for attention (and practice it)
    • 
          

        

      

    


    

      
Expectations: keep them few and clear

      
Three to five expectations, stated positively. Post them. Teach them. Refer to them often.

      

        
Good: "Respect people and property," "Come prepared," "Be ready to learn," "Help others"

        
Hard to enforce: "No talking," "Do not be late," "Be good," twelve rules on a poster

      

    


    

      
A simple response ladder

      
Use the same steps every time. Students learn quickly that your responses are predictable and fair.

      
    
        
  1. Quiet, private reminder (proximity, a look, a name next to the expectation)
    2. 
        
  2. Choice statement: "You can start the work now, or you can talk with me at break. Which one?"
    3. 
        
  3. Brief private conversation at a calm moment, not in front of peers
    4. 
        
  4. Follow your school's written process (referral, parent contact, admin support)
    5. 
      

      
Do not: argue, threaten consequences you cannot deliver, or make a public example of a student. Keep it calm and brief.

    


    

      
Positive reinforcement that works

      
    
        
  • Specific praise: "You checked your work before turning it in. That's a great habit." beats "Good job."
    • 
        
  • Catch students doing the right thing and name them, especially the students who usually get noticed for the wrong things.
    • 
        
  • Aim for more positive interactions than corrections. Many teachers find that a 4-to-1 ratio helps.
    • 
        
  • Make a quick positive call home early in the year. It changes the relationship before a problem starts.
    • 
      

    


    

      
When a lesson falls apart

      
    
        
  • Stop, breathe, and pause. Silence gets attention faster than volume.
    • 
        
  • Shorten the task or switch to a more active format (stand up, partner talk, whiteboards).
    • 
        
  • Give a clear, short reset: "Pencils down, eyes up. Here's what we're doing next."
    • 
        
  • Afterward, write down what happened and what to change. Do not beat yourself up.
    • 
      

    

  


  
  

    

      
Scripts and templates

      
Copy these and adjust them. Always check your school's communication policies before sending.

    


    

      
Parent emails

      

        First positive contact
        
Subject: Getting to know [Student's first name]

Hello [Parent/Guardian name],

I'm [Your name], [Student's first name]'s [subject/homeroom] teacher this year. I wanted to reach out early to say I'm glad to have [Student] in class.

[Specific positive detail, e.g., "Today [Student] helped a classmate understand the warm-up problem."]

Please feel free to reach me at [email/phone/preferred contact]. I look forward to working together.

Thank you,
[Your name]

      

      

        Concern about behavior or work
        
Subject: Checking in about [Student's first name]

Hello [Parent/Guardian name],

I'm reaching out because I've noticed [specific, factual observation, e.g., "[Student] has missed three homework assignments this week"]. I know [Student] is capable of more, and I'd like to work with you on a plan.

Could we find a time to talk this week? I'm available [times]. You can also reply here with any questions.

Thank you,
[Your name]

      

      

        Reply to an upset parent (pause, then respond)
        
Hello [Parent/Guardian name],

Thank you for telling me your concerns. I want to understand what happened and make sure [Student] gets the support they need.

I'd like to look at the details and talk with you. Would [day/time] or [alternative] work for a phone call or meeting? If it's easier, I can also call you after school today.

Best,
[Your name]

      

      
Rule of thumb: Wait a few minutes (or until the next day) before replying to an angry email. Stick to facts, and loop in your administrator if the situation escalates.

    


    

      
Sentences for hard moments with students

      

        
When a student refuses to work

        
"I can see something is making this hard right now. Can you tell me what it is, or would you like a minute first?"

        
When you need the student to stop something right away

        
"[Name], I need you to put the phone in the basket now. Thank you."

        
When you want to talk privately after class

        
"I'm not upset with you. I want to hear your side and figure out what's going on."

        
When you don't know the answer

        
"That's a great question. I don't know yet. Let me find out and I'll get back to you tomorrow."

        
When a student is off-topic

        
"I want to hear about that at lunch. Right now, let's get back to [the task]."

        
When you made a mistake

        
"I got that wrong. Here's the correct version. Thanks for catching it."

      

    


    

      
Asking colleagues and admin for help

      
Hi [Name], do you have 10 minutes this week? I'm working on [specific issue, e.g., "a plan for students who finish early"] and would love your perspective. I'd especially value [something specific they know about]. Thank you!

      
Hi [Admin name], I wanted to flag [situation] early so we can plan together. Here's what I've tried so far: [1-2 bullets]. What support can you offer, and what would you suggest next?

    

  


  
  

    

      
Time management

      
    
        
  • Batch your grading. Put it in set blocks instead of trickling it in all week.
    • 
        
  • Reuse what you can. Ask colleagues for their materials and share yours back.
    • 
        
  • Use a simple task list. Three must-dos each day. Anything else is a bonus.
    • 
        
  • Set an end-of-day shutdown routine: check tomorrow's plan, close tabs, leave.
    • 
        
  • Protect at least one evening and one weekend day each week.
    • 
      

    


    

      
Mental health and burnout

      
    
        
  • Feeling overwhelmed in year one is common. Persistent exhaustion, dread, or hopelessness is worth talking about with someone you trust.
    • 
        
  • Find one colleague you can vent to, and one who will help you problem-solve.
    • 
        
  • Keep a "good things" list. Write down one student moment each day, even a small one.
    • 
        
  • If you are struggling significantly, contact your school's counselor, employee assistance program, or your doctor. Asking is a sign of strength.
    • 
      

    


    

      
Who to go to for what

      

        

          
Classroom and lesson questions

          
Mentor, grade-level or department team, instructional coach

        

        

          
Student behavior or safety

          
Your administrator, counselor, behavior specialist. Follow your school's reporting process immediately for any safety concern.

        

        

          
Special education (IEP/504) questions

          
Case manager or special education coordinator

        

        

          
Tech, grades, or attendance systems

          
Tech support, front office, or your school's data specialist

        

        

          
Pay, contract, benefits, or leave

          
HR or your union representative, if you have one

        

        

          
Mandated reporting (abuse or neglect)

          
Follow your state's law and your district policy. Report to the proper authority promptly. Do not investigate it yourself.

        

      

    


    

      
Documentation habits

      
Keep a simple, dated log for anything important. Note the date, who was involved, what happened, what you did, and any follow-up. Store it somewhere consistent with your district's policy. Keep your notes factual and professional. Assume anything you write could be read by a parent or administrator.

Jargon decoder

      
Schools use a lot of acronyms. Terms vary by state and district, so confirm locally.

      

        
IEP (Individualized Education Program)

        
A legal plan for students with disabilities that receive special education services. Follow its accommodations exactly.

        
504 Plan

        
A plan for students with a disability who need accommodations but not specialized instruction.

        
MTSS / RTI

        
Multi-Tiered System of Supports, or Response to Intervention. A framework for providing increasing levels of support based on need.

        
PLC (Professional Learning Community)

        
A team that meets regularly to plan, analyze student data, and improve instruction.

        
Formative assessment

        
Checks during learning (exit tickets, quick questions) used to adjust teaching. Usually not graded heavily.

        
Summative assessment

        
An end-of-unit or end-of-course evaluation that measures learning.

        
Pacing guide

        
A calendar showing when to cover each unit or standard.

        
Standards (CCSS, TEKS, state standards)

        
The learning goals your state or district expects students to meet. Your curriculum should align with them.

        
ELL / EL / MLL

        
English Learner or Multilingual Learner. Students developing English skills who may need language supports.

        
Accommodation vs. modification

        
An accommodation changes how a student accesses learning (extra time, a quiet space). A modification changes what is expected. Check each student's plan.

        
Bell-ringer / warm-up / do-now

        
A short task students begin as soon as they enter, usually to settle them and activate prior knowledge.

        
Exit ticket

        
A brief question students answer before leaving, giving you quick feedback on what they learned.

        
Scaffolding

        
Temporary supports (sentence starters, examples, graphic organizers) that you gradually remove as students gain skill.

        
Mandated reporter

        
A teacher required by law to report suspected child abuse or neglect to the designated authority.
